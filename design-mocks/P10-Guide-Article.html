Soil pH, the complete guide — what it is, why it matters, and how to fix it.

Your soil's pH decides which nutrients your plants can actually absorb. It's the dial everything else turns on — and it's easier to adjust than most gardeners think.

Every gardening forum eventually ends up at the same complaint: "I'm feeding it, I'm watering it, and it still looks sick." Nine times out of ten, the problem isn't the fertilizer bag — it's the pH of the soil holding it. Nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, manganese: all of them lock away when the pH drifts. Fix that dial, and the rest of the garden often fixes itself.

What soil pH actually measures

pH is a measure of how acidic or alkaline your soil is — on a logarithmic scale from 0 (strong acid) to 14 (strong base), with 7.0 being neutral. Because it's logarithmic, each full point is a ten-fold change: a pH of 5.0 is ten times more acidic than 6.0, and a hundred times more acidic than 7.0.

Soil is typically in the 4.5–8.5 range. Forests and conifer-heavy areas drift acidic (4.5–6.0). Arid-region soils and those sitting on limestone run alkaline (7.5–8.5). Most established vegetable gardens land in the 6.0–6.8 sweet spot — which is, not coincidentally, where most crops want to live.

Why it matters more than fertilizer

At the wrong pH, nutrients don't move the way the chemistry diagrams say they should. Below 6.0, phosphorus binds to iron and aluminum and becomes unavailable. Above 7.5, iron and manganese lock up, which is why alkaline-soil gardeners see yellowing leaves that more nitrogen never fixes.

You can pour a 50-pound bag of 10-10-10 onto a pH 5.0 bed and most of the phosphorus will sit there, chemically locked, doing nothing. — NCSU Extension, Soils & Fertility Factsheet

That's the unglamorous reason pH is worth testing before you spend money on amendments. The fertilizer aisle doesn't tell you that a $3 pH test can save you $40 in product you won't need.

How to test accurately

There are three tiers of pH testing. They are not equivalent, and the gap between them matters:

  1. Hardware-store strip or probe meter — ballpark only. Good for catching "I'm way off" (pH 5 vs pH 8) but unreliable for the quarter-point distinctions that actually drive amendment decisions.
  2. Home test kit (LaMotte, Rapitest) — better. Titration-style kits read to the tenth, but only on a fresh soil slurry with distilled water. Tap water will throw the reading.
  3. University Extension soil test — authoritative. $15–25. Returns pH along with CEC, OM%, and macro/micronutrients, with amendment recommendations calibrated to your soil texture. This is the only tier you should base a liming plan on.
Quick answer If you're buying a test for a one-time amendment decision, skip the meter aisle and send a sample to your state Extension service. The Cornell, UMN, and Penn State programs each cost under $25 and return results in 1–2 weeks.

How to raise pH — liming the soil

To raise pH, you add a base — usually agricultural lime (calcium carbonate). The dose depends on three things: how far you're trying to move the pH, your soil's texture (clay resists change; sand moves fast), and the bed's area.

lime_lbs = (target_pH − current_pH) × area_ft² × factor factor: 1.0 sandy · 1.6 loam · 2.3 clay (per UMass Ext., lb per ft² per pH point)

A 100 ft² loam bed moving from 5.2 → 6.5 needs about 21 pounds of calcitic lime — roughly half a 40-pound bag. The soil pH calculator handles the math plus the sulfur equivalent for lowering pH.

How to lower pH — acidifying the soil

To lower pH, you add an acidifier — elemental sulfur is the standard. Sulfur works slower than lime (it needs soil bacteria to oxidize it to sulfuric acid), so plan for 3–9 months, and never apply more than 2 lb per 100 ft² in a single pass.

If you're trying to lower pH below 5.5 to grow blueberries, the honest answer from most Extension services is: consider raised beds filled with peat-based mix instead. Holding a native alkaline soil below pH 5.5 is a multi-year, multi-application commitment.

Don't Don't use aluminum sulfate to acidify food-growing beds. It drops pH fast but leaves excess aluminum, which is toxic to many vegetable roots. Save aluminum sulfate for hydrangeas.

When to apply — and how long to wait

Fall is the right time for both amendments. Lime takes 3–6 months to fully react; sulfur needs 6–9 months in warm soil, longer in cool. If you apply in April, you won't see the finished pH until the crop is already planted.

Target pH by crop

Most vegetables are happy at 6.0–6.8. The exceptions are worth knowing:

CropTarget pHNotes
Blueberries4.5–5.5The big outlier — needs sustained acid
Potatoes5.0–6.0Lower pH suppresses scab
Tomato, pepper6.0–6.8Standard sweet spot
Leafy greens6.0–7.0Tolerant; lettuce handles up to 7.2
Asparagus6.5–7.5Prefers the alkaline side
Brassicas6.5–7.2Higher pH suppresses clubroot

Common mistakes

  • Applying lime in spring when the crop is already in. Lime takes months to react; by the time it's worked, the season is over.
  • Using the same rate on sand and clay. Clay holds pH tenaciously. A rate that works on sand will barely move clay, and twice that rate on sand will over-correct.
  • Trusting a single hardware-store probe reading. Probe meters drift, and a dry-soil reading lies. Always rewet the soil and wait 30 seconds.
  • Liming every year out of habit. Once pH is in range, retest on a 2–3 year cycle. Lime doesn't wear off the way mulch does.
The short version Test before you amend. Apply in fall. Use the formula. Retest before the next amendment. Most pH problems are solved in one cycle if you don't guess on the dose.
